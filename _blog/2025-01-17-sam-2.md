## Paper

[SAM 2: Segment Anything in Images and Videos](https://arxiv.org/abs/2408.00714) — Ravi et al., 2024

## Core idea

SAM 2 unifies promptable segmentation for images and videos. Its defining component is a streaming-memory architecture that uses information from previous frames and user interactions to track a target through a video. A data engine supports iterative collection and refinement of video masks, extending the scale-oriented approach of the original SAM beyond static images.

This is more than running SAM independently on every frame. Memory gives the model a representation of what has already been identified, allowing prompts in one frame to influence predictions elsewhere in the sequence.

## Why it mattered

Temporal consistency is a major limitation of frame-wise segmentation. A model can produce plausible masks in individual frames yet change object identity, shape, or boundary abruptly over time. SAM 2 treats consistency and interaction as part of the task rather than as post-processing.

The unified image–video formulation is also important. A single promptable model can handle a static image as a one-frame sequence while scaling to interactive tracking, reducing the separation between image segmentation and video object segmentation.

## Medical imaging perspective

It is tempting to reinterpret a CT or MR volume as a video whose frames are axial slices. The analogy is useful because neighboring slices share anatomy, and memory may help maintain a structure when its boundary becomes faint. It could also reduce annotation effort if a prompt on one slice propagates through a volume.

But a volume is not simply a video. Slice order represents space rather than time, voxel spacing may be anisotropic, and information should often propagate in both directions. Clinical volumes can also contain abrupt anatomical appearances caused by partial-volume effects or acquisition gaps. A memory strategy designed for temporal tracking may carry an incorrect mask through these transitions.

For dynamic angiography or perfusion imaging, the analogy changes again: the sequence has a true temporal dimension, sometimes in addition to 3D space. A useful medical adaptation must distinguish spatial continuity from temporal dynamics instead of treating every ordered set of images identically.

## Takeaway

SAM 2 suggests that memory and interaction are valuable ingredients for medical segmentation, particularly for annotation across slices or time points. Its main lesson is architectural rather than plug-and-play: preserving context across a sequence can improve consistency, but medical data require geometry-, spacing-, and modality-aware memory mechanisms.
